Employment & Skills

Shortages are easing — but the gaps that matter most persist

June 2026

Jobs and Skills Australia's 2025 Occupation Shortage List found about 29% of assessed occupations in national shortage — down from 33% in 2024 and 36% in 2023. The relief is uneven: nearly half of trade roles and two in five professional roles remain short, and 139 occupations have now been in shortage for five straight years, concentrated in construction, engineering, health and early-childhood education. For employers, the hardest-to-fill roles are structural, not temporary.

Source: Jobs and Skills Australia, 2025 Occupation Shortage List.
Construction

Australia's housing target meets a workforce reality

May 2026

The national goal of 1.2 million new homes by 2029 is running into a shortage of skilled trades. Master Builders Australia reported construction apprenticeship completions fell about 15% and commencements dropped around 22% in a single year — thinning the pipeline just as demand peaks. Builders who plan their workforce early and invest in training and retention will be the ones who keep projects moving.

Source: Master Builders Australia; Jobs and Skills Australia; ABC News.
Aged Care & Health

The care economy's demographic wave

April 2026

Australia will need an estimated 110,000 additional direct aged-care workers within a decade, rising toward 400,000 by 2050, as the share of people over 65 approaches one in five by 2031. Aged and disability carers are projected to be among the fastest-growing occupations in the country. Attracting — and, crucially, keeping — these workers is now one of the defining workforce challenges of the decade.

Source: CEDA, Duty of Care (2025); ABS population projections.
Regional Workforce

Where shortages bite hardest: regional Australia

March 2026

Twenty-one occupations were in shortage in regional Australia alone in 2025. Regional vacancy fill rates have improved — to about 68.6%, the strongest gain in years — yet city employers still fill roles more easily. Housing, distance and thinner local applicant pools make regional hiring uniquely hard. Long-term settlement support, not just recruitment, is what makes regional placements last.

Source: Jobs and Skills Australia, 2025.
Future of Work

Diversity and technology: the quiet workforce advantage

February 2026

Jobs and Skills Australia found that occupations with stronger participation from women, older workers, First Nations people and people with disability are far less likely to be in shortage — reframing inclusion as a workforce-resilience strategy, not only an equity goal. At the same time, AI and automation are reshaping tasks across every sector. The employers who thrive will widen their talent pools and invest in skills that complement new technology.

Source: Jobs and Skills Australia, 2025.

For employers

Workforce planning for the season ahead
Start with the roles, not the people: list each position you will need, how many, from when, and for how long. Check the award pay floor for each role before budgeting, decide early whether accommodation or transport support is needed for regional sites, and build a two-to-three month lead time for sourcing and onboarding. A clear plan shared with your recruitment partner is the single biggest predictor of positions filled on time.
Writing a role brief that attracts the right people
The best briefs answer four questions in plain words: what the person will actually do each day; the pay and how it is set; the roster, including weekends and overtime expectations; and the level of English genuinely needed for the job. Be honest about the hard parts — heat, early starts, physical work. Honest briefs attract workers who stay; polished briefs that hide the reality attract workers who leave.
Onboarding overseas-recruited staff so they stay
The first month decides the first year. Have housing, transport to site, a local phone plan and banking sorted before day one. Assign a buddy for the first fortnight, walk through payslips in the first pay cycle so nothing is a surprise, and check in at week one, week four and month three. Workers who settle well become your most loyal people — and your best recruiters.

For workers

Getting ready to work in Australia
Prepare your documents early: passport, CV, qualification certificates, written references from past employers, and any English test results. Many jobs need specific tickets — a White Card for construction sites, a forklift licence for warehouses — and getting them early makes you easier to place. Keep clear photos or scans of everything in one folder on your phone.
Your first month: settling in well
In your first weeks, set up the essentials in this order: a local SIM and phone number, a bank account, your tax file number, and your superannuation fund details for your employer. Learn your route to work before your first shift. Find your local shops, clinic and community groups — and if anything at work is unclear, ask early; good employers would always rather answer a question than fix a problem.
Understanding pay basics in Australia
Most jobs in Australia are covered by an award — a legal document setting minimum pay for each type of work, including higher rates for overtime, weekends and public holidays. You must receive a payslip for every pay showing your hours, rate and superannuation. If you want to check a rate, the Fair Work Ombudsman website has a free pay calculator in many languages.

Hospitality & food services

Kitchens remain among the hardest teams to staff in regional Australia — cooks and chefs top demand lists year-round, with venues increasingly planning recruitment seasons ahead rather than reacting to vacancies.

Horticulture & fresh produce

Harvest and packing seasons drive predictable surges in demand for reliable workers, with growers who arrange accommodation and transport consistently filling crews faster than those who leave workers to arrange their own.
